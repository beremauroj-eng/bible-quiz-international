What is Jonah about?

Jonah is a compact prophetic narrative about a reluctant messenger sent to Nineveh. The prophet flees by sea, acknowledges responsibility for a storm, prays from a great fish and eventually delivers a brief warning. Nineveh responds, but Jonah resents mercy, leading to a plant, a worm and an unanswered divine question about compassion.

A four-chapter reading map

  1. Jonah 1 — flight and storm: Jonah heads toward Tarshish, sleeps during danger, is identified by lot and thrown into the sea at his request.
  2. Jonah 2 — prayer from the depths: the great fish contains Jonah, whose poetic prayer recalls distress, deliverance and renewed vows.
  3. Jonah 3 — mission and response: Jonah receives the command again, announces forty days and Nineveh responds from citizens to king.
  4. Jonah 4 — anger and compassion: Jonah objects to mercy, watches the city, values a shade plant and is questioned about God’s concern.

Sample questions and answers

Watch the narrative’s contrasts: pagan sailors and Ninevites respond quickly, while the prophet who knows God’s character repeatedly resists its implications.

Where did Jonah attempt to flee instead of going to Nineveh?
Answer: Toward Tarshish
Reference: Jonah 1:1–3

He travelled to Joppa and boarded a ship going away from the assigned mission.

What identified Jonah as connected with the storm?
Answer: The sailors cast lots, and the lot fell on Jonah
Reference: Jonah 1:7–12

Jonah then described his identity and told them that throwing him overboard would calm the sea.

How long was Jonah inside the great fish?
Answer: Three days and three nights
Reference: Jonah 1:17–2:1

From inside the fish Jonah prayed before being deposited on dry land.

What time limit did Jonah announce to Nineveh?
Answer: Forty days before Nineveh would be overthrown
Reference: Jonah 3:1–5

The people believed the warning and proclaimed a fast.

What destroyed the plant that had shaded Jonah?
Answer: A worm
Reference: Jonah 4:6–8

After the plant withered, a scorching east wind intensified Jonah’s distress.

What population does God mention in the final question about concern for Nineveh?
Answer: More than 120,000 people unable to distinguish right from left, as well as many animals
Reference: Jonah 4:10–11

The book ends without recording Jonah’s answer to this question about compassion.

How to study Jonah

Make a response chart for Jonah, sailors, sea, fish, Ninevites, plant, worm and wind. Note who obeys immediately and who resists. Track repeated verbs of arising, going down, appointing and relenting, and compare Jonah’s prayer with psalm language. Read the final chapter as essential rather than an epilogue: the central conflict is not only whether Jonah reaches Nineveh, but whether he shares concern for its people.

Context and interpretation note

Jonah’s genre, historical setting, use of irony and the nature of the great fish are debated. The Hebrew term does not identify a modern species, and the story’s theological purpose should not be reduced to zoology. Nineveh was associated with imperial violence, which helps explain—but does not automatically justify—Jonah’s anger. The quiz tests narrative actions, words and explicit numbers while avoiding anti-Jewish stereotypes or claims that one genre theory is certain.
