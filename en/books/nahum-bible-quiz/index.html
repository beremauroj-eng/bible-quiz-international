What is Nahum about?

Nahum is an oracle concerning Nineveh, capital of the Assyrian Empire. Its poetry announces the fall of a power remembered for conquest, deportation and brutality. A hymn to divine justice opens into vivid siege scenes and a taunt over the city’s collapse, presenting relief for Judah while forcing readers to confront violent judgment imagery.

A three-part reading map

  1. Nahum 1 — hymn and announcement: divine jealousy, patience and power, creation shaken, refuge for those who trust and good news that oppression will end.
  2. Nahum 2 — the fall pictured: attacker advancing, shields and chariots, breached defences, plunder and the emptied lion’s den.
  3. Nahum 3 — charges and taunt: bloodshed, deception and exploitation are exposed; Nineveh is compared with Thebes and declared beyond recovery.

Sample questions and answers

Read the battle images as prophetic poetry about an ancient empire; their emotional force comes from the experience of peoples subjected to Assyrian violence.

Against which city is Nahum’s oracle directed?
Answer: Nineveh
Reference: Nahum 1:1

The heading also identifies the work as the vision of Nahum the Elkoshite.

How does Nahum combine patience and power in describing the Lord?
Answer: The Lord is slow to anger yet great in power and does not leave guilt unaddressed
Reference: Nahum 1:2–3

The hymn then depicts whirlwind, storm, sea and mountains responding to divine presence.

What good news is announced to Judah in the first chapter?
Answer: Peace and the ending of the oppressor’s passage through the land
Reference: Nahum 1:12–15

A messenger on the mountains calls Judah to celebrate and fulfil its vows.

What animal image describes Assyria’s former store of prey?
Answer: A lion’s den
Reference: Nahum 2:11–13

The taunt asks where the lions fed and stored prey before divine opposition emptied the site.

Which conquered city is used to challenge Nineveh’s sense of security?
Answer: Thebes, also called No-Amon
Reference: Nahum 3:8–10

Thebes had water and allies yet still went into captivity.

How do the nations respond to news of Nineveh’s final wound?
Answer: They clap their hands
Reference: Nahum 3:18–19

The book ends by asking who had not experienced Assyria’s relentless cruelty.

How to study Nahum

Locate Assyria, Nineveh, Judah and Thebes and review Assyria’s regional dominance before reading the taunts. Mark storm, flood, lion, chariot and wound imagery, then identify the historical behaviour each image answers. Compare the opening character statement with Exodus 34 and Jonah 4, noting both mercy and accountability. Read all three chapters together so relief for victims and the horror of warfare remain visible.

Context and interpretation note

Nahum’s celebratory tone and graphic siege language are ethically difficult. The poetry addresses a specific violent empire and must not be redirected toward modern ethnic, national or religious groups. Details of the siege and the prophet’s exact date are reconstructed with varying confidence. The quiz tests the ancient city, images and stated accusations without encouraging revenge, denying the suffering involved in war or treating every contemporary enemy as a new Nineveh.
