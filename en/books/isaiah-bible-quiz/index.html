What is Isaiah about?

Isaiah brings together prophetic warnings, historical narratives, songs and visions spanning judgment and hope. Addressed first to Judah and Jerusalem amid imperial threats, the book confronts injustice, misplaced alliances and empty worship while announcing purification, a faithful remnant, a coming ruler, servant figures, return from exile and renewed creation.

A five-part reading map

  1. Isaiah 1–12 — Judah, calling and promised ruler: covenant charges, Isaiah’s temple vision, the Syro-Ephraimite crisis, Immanuel signs and songs of deliverance.
  2. Isaiah 13–27 — nations and worldwide judgment: oracles concerning surrounding powers, the fall of proud cities and hope beyond devastation.
  3. Isaiah 28–39 — warnings and Hezekiah: unreliable alliances, the Assyrian invasion, Jerusalem’s deliverance, Hezekiah’s illness and Babylonian envoys.
  4. Isaiah 40–55 — comfort, return and servant: God’s incomparable rule, Cyrus, challenges to idols, servant songs and invitation to renewed covenant life.
  5. Isaiah 56–66 — restored community and new creation: justice, true worship, Zion’s future, an anointed herald and new heavens and earth.

Sample questions and answers

Place each passage within its historical horizon before connecting it to later interpretation; Isaiah often moves between immediate crisis, future restoration and expansive poetic hope.

What did the seraphim proclaim in Isaiah’s temple vision?
Answer: “Holy, holy, holy is the Lord of hosts,” with the whole earth full of his glory
Reference: Isaiah 6:1–4

The vision occurs in the year of King Uzziah’s death and leads to Isaiah’s commission.

What name was given to the child announced as a sign during Ahaz’s crisis?
Answer: Immanuel
Reference: Isaiah 7:10–17

The name means “God with us” and appears within a warning involving the two threatening kings.

Which king took the Assyrian threat into the temple and prayed?
Answer: Hezekiah
Reference: Isaiah 37:1–20

Hezekiah spread Sennacherib’s letter before the Lord and appealed for deliverance.

What message opens the new section beginning in Isaiah 40?
Answer: “Comfort, comfort my people”
Reference: Isaiah 40:1–5

The passage announces preparation of a way in the wilderness and revelation of divine glory.

How does Isaiah 53 describe the servant’s relation to the wounds and wrongdoing of others?
Answer: He was wounded for their transgressions and bore their iniquities
Reference: Isaiah 52:13–53:12

The servant suffers, is vindicated and is said to make many righteous.

What does God promise to create near the end of Isaiah?
Answer: New heavens and a new earth
Reference: Isaiah 65:17–25; 66:22

The vision describes transformed joy, life and worship while contrasting final outcomes.

How to study Isaiah

Build a timeline for Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah alongside Assyria and Babylon. Mark recurring images—vineyard, highway, remnant, branch, servant, Zion and light—and watch how their meaning develops. Read the prose history in chapters 36–39 beside 2 Kings 18–20. For a passage quoted later in Scripture, first record its original speaker, audience and crisis, then compare how the later writer reuses it.

Context and interpretation note

Isaiah’s composition, historical layers, servant identity and fulfilment patterns are extensively debated. Hebrew, Greek and Dead Sea Scroll readings sometimes differ, and poetic future language should not be flattened into a single timetable. Jewish and Christian traditions also interpret royal and servant texts differently.
