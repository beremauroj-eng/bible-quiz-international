What is Psalms about?

Psalms is a collection of 150 poetic prayers and songs arranged in five books. It gives language for praise, lament, thanksgiving, confession, wisdom, royal hope and communal memory. Individual headings associate many psalms with David or other figures and settings, while the collection as a whole moves toward sustained praise in its final sequence.

A five-book reading map

  1. Psalms 1–41 — Book One: an opening contrast between two ways, many Davidic prayers and repeated movement from distress to trust.
  2. Psalms 42–72 — Book Two: Korahite and Davidic collections, longing for God, royal prayer and a closing notice about David’s prayers.
  3. Psalms 73–89 — Book Three: sanctuary reflection, national crisis, covenant questions and the dark conclusion of Psalm 88.
  4. Psalms 90–106 — Book Four: Moses, divine kingship, creation, wilderness memory and renewed praise.
  5. Psalms 107–150 — Book Five: thanksgiving, Songs of Ascents, Torah meditation and five concluding hallelujah psalms.

Sample questions and answers

Read the heading, speaker, audience and emotional movement of each psalm; similar images can serve different purposes in lament, praise or instruction.

What image describes the person who delights in the Lord’s instruction in Psalm 1?
Answer: A tree planted beside streams of water
Reference: Psalm 1:1–3

The tree yields fruit in season and its leaf does not wither.

What role does the Lord take in the opening line of Psalm 23?
Answer: Shepherd
Reference: Psalm 23:1–4

The shepherd image develops through provision, guidance and protection.

What event does the heading of Psalm 51 connect with David’s confession?
Answer: Nathan confronting David after David went to Bathsheba
Reference: Psalm 51 heading; 2 Samuel 12:1–13

The psalm asks for mercy, cleansing and a renewed heart.

Who is named in the heading of Psalm 90?
Answer: Moses, the man of God
Reference: Psalm 90 heading

The prayer contrasts human brevity with God’s enduring existence.

What subject is celebrated throughout the long acrostic Psalm 119?
Answer: God’s word, instruction, commands and related terms
Reference: Psalm 119

Its twenty-two sections follow the Hebrew alphabet and repeatedly describe devotion to divine instruction.

What is invited to praise the Lord in the final verse of Psalm 150?
Answer: Everything that has breath
Reference: Psalm 150:1–6

The collection ends with a comprehensive call to praise.

How to study Psalms

Classify a psalm provisionally—lament, praise, thanksgiving, wisdom, royal or another form—then trace its movement rather than extracting one line. Mark parallel lines that repeat, contrast or extend an idea. Use the five-book divisions and final doxologies to see editorial structure. Compare repeated psalms and quotations elsewhere in Scripture, but keep the original voice and setting visible before considering later interpretation or personal application.

Translation and interpretation note

Hebrew poetry depends on parallelism, imagery and sound that cannot transfer completely into English. Psalm numbering differs between Hebrew-based and Greek/Latin traditions, and headings, musical terms such as “Selah,” superscriptions and authorship are interpreted differently. Imprecatory language and royal or messianic readings also require context. Quiz references follow the site’s displayed numbering and test the text or heading cited without claiming that every genre, historical setting or later theological use is uncontested.
