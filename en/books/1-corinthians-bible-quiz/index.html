What is 1 Corinthians about?

Paul writes to a gifted but divided Corinthian church. He answers reports and questions about factions, sexuality, lawsuits, marriage, food offered to idols, worship, spiritual gifts, resurrection and collection. The letter connects the message of the cross with embodied communal conduct.

This guide treats 1 Corinthians as a situated argument rather than a collection of slogans. The six questions sample distinct units, while the map locates every answer. Watch repetitions, contrasts, connective words and shifts of audience. Verify an answer across more than one verse whenever its wording depends on the reasoning before or after it.

A four-part reading map

  1. 1–4 — cross and factions: rival loyalties, God’s wisdom and apostolic service
  2. 5–7 — bodies and relationships: discipline, lawsuits, sexuality and marriage
  3. 8–14 — freedom and worship: idol food, Lord’s Supper, gifts and ordered participation
  4. 15–16 — resurrection and mission: witnesses, transformed bodies, collection and coworkers

Sample questions and answers

Use each reference as an entry point and read the whole unit before selecting an answer.

Who planted and who watered?
Answer: Paul planted and Apollos watered
Reference: 1 Corinthians 3:5–9

The agricultural image makes God responsible for growth.

What should a person do before eating the Lord’s Supper?
Answer: Examine oneself
Reference: 1 Corinthians 11:27–32

The instruction follows criticism of humiliating poorer members.

What image describes believers with many gifts?
Answer: One body with many members
Reference: 1 Corinthians 12:12–27

Difference and mutual dependence belong together.

What does Paul call the greatest of faith, hope and love?
Answer: Love
Reference: 1 Corinthians 13:13

The poem sits inside correction about competitive gift use.

What tradition did Paul receive about Christ?
Answer: He died, was buried, was raised and appeared
Reference: 1 Corinthians 15:1–8

Paul lists named witnesses as part of the resurrection proclamation.

What collection should be prepared weekly?
Answer: The collection for God’s people
Reference: 1 Corinthians 16:1–4

Regular setting aside would prevent a last-minute appeal.

How to study 1 Corinthians

Read 1 Corinthians as a complete letter before isolating memorable lines. Summarise each of the four mapped sections and note who speaks, which problem is answered and what practical consequence follows. Trace connections among cross and factions, bodies and relationships, freedom and worship, resurrection and mission. For every question, verify the full paragraph, compare transitions and distinguish assertion, quotation, metaphor and exhortation. Record repeated terms and named coworkers, then explain how the closing material returns to concerns introduced near the opening. This method keeps recall, argument, social setting and application in conversation.

Context and interpretation note

Interpretations differ on discipline, gender, marriage, tongues, prophecy and the resurrection body. Chapter 13 must not be detached from chapters 12 and 14, and chapter 11 must be read with its social setting. The quiz reports Paul’s arguments without turning one denominational practice into the only faithful application.
