What is Joel about?

Joel begins with an agricultural catastrophe described through successive locust swarms and drought. The disaster becomes a summons to communal lament, fasting and wholehearted return. The book then expands the “day of the Lord” beyond Judah’s crisis, promising restoration, the outpouring of God’s Spirit and judgment among the nations.

A four-part reading map

  1. Joel 1 — devastation and lament: elders and generations are called to remember, crops fail, worship supplies disappear and priests summon a sacred assembly.
  2. Joel 2:1–17 — alarm and return: an overwhelming army-like invasion approaches, yet the people are called to rend hearts rather than garments.
  3. Joel 2:18–32 — restoration and Spirit: grain and wine return, shame is removed, the Spirit is poured on all kinds of people and cosmic signs appear.
  4. Joel 3 — nations and Zion: peoples gather for judgment, weapons are forged, harvest images signal decision and Jerusalem receives renewed security.

Sample questions and answers

Check chapter numbering in your Bible, because Hebrew, Catholic and some English traditions divide parts of Joel differently even when the wording is the same.

What disaster opens Joel’s call to remember and tell future generations?
Answer: Successive locust swarms consuming the land
Reference: Joel 1:1–4

The repeated locust terms emphasise comprehensive loss and introduce the communal lament.

Where were the priests told to weep while pleading for the people?
Answer: Between the porch or vestibule and the altar
Reference: Joel 2:15–17

The sacred assembly includes elders, children and newly married people.

What are the people told to tear instead of their garments?
Answer: Their hearts
Reference: Joel 2:12–13

The appeal grounds return in God’s gracious, compassionate and patient character.

On whom does God promise to pour out the Spirit?
Answer: All flesh, including sons, daughters, old, young and servants
Reference: Joel 2:28–29

The promise crosses age, gender and social status and includes prophecy, dreams and visions.

What celestial signs precede the great day of the Lord?
Answer: The sun turns to darkness and the moon to blood
Reference: Joel 2:30–32

The signs accompany the promise that those calling on the Lord’s name will be saved.

What are the nations told to make from ploughshares and pruning hooks?
Answer: Swords and spears
Reference: Joel 3:9–13

The reversal of peace imagery summons the nations to judgment in the valley.

How to study Joel

Trace the movement from field to temple to nations: locust loss affects farmers, worship and the whole community before the horizon widens. Mark every imperative calling people to wake, mourn, fast, gather or return. Compare the army language in chapter 2 with the locust imagery without assuming too quickly that every interpreter identifies them identically. Then follow how restoration repeats and reverses the opening losses.

Context and interpretation note

Joel does not name a king, so its date is disputed. Interpreters also differ over whether the invading force is locust, human army, apocalyptic image or a layered combination. Chapter and verse numbering varies, and later biblical quotations extend the Spirit promise into new contexts. The quiz tests images and statements in Joel’s cited passages without assigning a certain date or converting cosmic poetry into a prediction schedule.
