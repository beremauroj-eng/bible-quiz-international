What is Ezekiel about?

Ezekiel records dated visions, symbolic actions and prophetic speeches among Judean exiles in Babylon. The prophet announces Jerusalem’s judgment, confronts corrupt leadership and performs embodied signs. After the city falls, the emphasis turns toward responsible watchmanship, a new heart and spirit, national restoration, defeat of hostile powers and an immense temple vision.

A five-part reading map

  1. Ezekiel 1–3 — vision and commission: living creatures, wheels, divine throne, eaten scroll and appointment as watchman.
  2. Ezekiel 4–24 — signs and Jerusalem’s judgment: siege enactments, temple abominations, responsibility, allegories, leaders and the announced fall of the city.
  3. Ezekiel 25–32 — oracles against nations: messages concerning Ammon, Moab, Edom, Philistia, Tyre, Sidon and Egypt.
  4. Ezekiel 33–39 — news and restoration: renewed watchman role, failed shepherds, new heart, dry bones, reunited sticks and Gog’s defeat.
  5. Ezekiel 40–48 — temple, land and river: measured sacred complex, returning glory, priestly order, territorial allotment and life-giving water.

Sample questions and answers

Record dates, locations and whether a scene is vision, sign-act, allegory or direct speech; Ezekiel’s vivid images are not all literal descriptions of the same event.

Which four faces did each living creature have in Ezekiel’s opening vision?
Answer: Human, lion, ox and eagle
Reference: Ezekiel 1:4–14

The creatures move with the spirit beneath the expanse supporting the throne vision.

What was Ezekiel told to eat before speaking to Israel?
Answer: A scroll containing lamentation, mourning and woe
Reference: Ezekiel 2:8–3:3

Although its contents were severe, the scroll tasted sweet as honey in his mouth.

What responsibility did the watchman image place on Ezekiel?
Answer: To hear God’s warning and deliver it to the people
Reference: Ezekiel 3:16–21; 33:1–9

The watchman is accountable for warning, while hearers remain responsible for their response.

What does God promise to replace the heart of stone with?
Answer: A heart of flesh, together with a new spirit
Reference: Ezekiel 36:24–28

The promise appears within cleansing, return and renewed obedience.

What happened when Ezekiel prophesied to the dry bones?
Answer: The bones joined, received bodies and breath, and stood as a vast army
Reference: Ezekiel 37:1–14

The vision interprets the bones as the house of Israel in despair and promises restoration.

What effect did the river from the restored temple have as it flowed east?
Answer: It deepened and brought fresh water, abundant life and healing trees
Reference: Ezekiel 47:1–12

The river transforms the waters it reaches and supports fruit-bearing trees along its banks.

How to study Ezekiel

Construct a dated timeline from the exile of King Jehoiachin and mark the report of Jerusalem’s fall as the book’s turning point. Keep separate columns for visions, enacted signs, allegories and explanatory speeches. Sketch the movement of divine glory in chapters 1, 8–11 and 43. For chapters 40–48, record measurements and functions without assuming in advance whether the vision is architectural, symbolic, eschatological or some combination.

Context and interpretation note

Ezekiel contains disturbing violence, sexualised allegory, trauma and priestly purity language that requires careful handling. Symbolic measurements, Gog and Magog, individual responsibility and the restored temple receive widely different Jewish and Christian interpretations. Ancient place names and textual readings may also vary. The quiz tests people, images and explicit explanations in cited passages; it does not turn contested symbolism into a single end-times chart or use harsh metaphors to excuse abuse.
