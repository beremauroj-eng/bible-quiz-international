What is Obadiah about?

A short oracle concerning Edom, violence and the reversal of pride.

This page brings together selected questions about Obadiah, with context, references and links for further study. The aim is not to impose a doctrinal interpretation, but to help you recognise what appears in the text and verify each answer in its cited passage.

Sample questions and answers

Obadiah has only twenty-one verses, so each question points to a precise line of the book rather than relying on general Old Testament trivia.

Against which nation is Obadiah’s vision directed?
Answer: Edom
Reference: Obadiah 1:1

The opening identifies Edom as the subject of the announced judgment.

What had deceived Edom according to verse 3?
Answer: The pride of its heart
Reference: Obadiah 1:3

The warning connects Edom’s confidence with its high and defensible dwelling places.

How did Edom respond when strangers carried off Jerusalem’s wealth?
Answer: It stood aloof
Reference: Obadiah 1:11

The verse treats Edom’s refusal to help as participation in the wrongdoing.

What principle of recompense appears in verse 15?
Answer: “As you have done, it shall be done to you.”
Reference: Obadiah 1:15

The statement is placed within the announcement of the day of the Lord upon the nations.

Where does Obadiah say there will be deliverance?
Answer: Mount Zion
Reference: Obadiah 1:17

The final movement contrasts Mount Zion’s deliverance with the judgment announced against Edom.

What closing declaration ends the book?
Answer: “The kingdom shall be the Lord’s.”
Reference: Obadiah 1:21

This declaration concludes the vision after the reference to deliverers going up on Mount Zion.

A three-part reading map

  1. Verses 1–9: Edom’s pride, false security and coming loss.
  2. Verses 10–14: violence against Jacob and a sequence of warnings about Edom’s conduct during Jerusalem’s disaster.
  3. Verses 15–21: the day of the Lord, recompense, Mount Zion and the book’s closing declaration.

Names and relationships to track

Edom is associated with Esau, while Jacob represents the related people of Israel. That family relationship makes the accusation of violence especially important to the book’s argument. Obadiah also names Mount Esau and Mount Zion, using geography to organise the contrast between judgment and deliverance.

How to study Obadiah

Read the entire book aloud—it is short enough to hear as one unit. On a second reading, mark every command beginning with “do not” in verses 12–14. Then trace the movement from Edom’s high dwelling place to Mount Zion. Finally, answer the quiz from memory and return to the exact verse for each missed item. This method tests sequence, repeated language and relationships rather than only the book’s position in the canon.

Translation note

English translations may differ in their rendering of particular verbs and place names. The quiz therefore gives verse references and focuses on details that remain identifiable across commonly used translations. Historical questions about the precise date and event behind the oracle are treated cautiously because scholarly proposals differ.
