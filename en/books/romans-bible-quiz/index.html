What is Romans about?

Romans is Paul’s sustained letter to assemblies in the imperial capital, presenting his gospel and seeking support for further mission. It argues that Jews and Gentiles alike need God’s righteousness and that God’s faithfulness is revealed through Jesus Christ. The letter moves from sin and justification through new life, Israel’s place and transformed communal practice.

A four-part reading map

  1. Romans 1–4 — gospel, sin and faith: programmatic theme, universal accountability, God’s righteousness, boasting excluded and Abraham as example.
  2. Romans 5–8 — new humanity and Spirit: Adam and Christ, baptism, conflict with sin, liberation and hope within suffering creation.
  3. Romans 9–11 — Israel and the nations: anguish, election, responsibility, proclamation, olive-tree imagery and a concluding doxology.
  4. Romans 12–16 — transformed community and mission: living sacrifice, diverse gifts, love, authorities, disputed practices, unity, travel plans and coworkers.

Sample questions and answers

Follow Paul’s questions and imagined objections; individual verses often answer an argument developed over several chapters.

What does Paul call the power of God for salvation?
Answer: The gospel
Reference: Romans 1:14–17

The programmatic statement says it reveals God’s righteousness by faith.

What was credited to Abraham as righteousness?
Answer: His faith or trusting God
Reference: Romans 4:1–12

Paul uses Abraham before circumcision to address both Jewish and Gentile inclusion.

What contrast closes Romans 6?
Answer: Sin’s wages are death, but God’s gift is eternal life in Christ
Reference: Romans 6:20–23

The contrast concludes an argument about freedom, allegiance and transformed life.

What declaration opens Romans 8?
Answer: There is now no condemnation for those in Christ Jesus
Reference: Romans 8:1–4

The passage explains liberation through the Spirit from sin and death.

What does Paul urge believers to present as a living sacrifice?
Answer: Their bodies
Reference: Romans 12:1–2

The appeal connects worship with transformed thinking and discernment.

Who is commended as a servant or deacon of the church at Cenchreae?
Answer: Phoebe
Reference: Romans 16:1–2

Paul asks the Roman believers to welcome and assist her.

How to study Romans

Outline each argumentative section and write Paul’s question, answer and scriptural evidence. Track “therefore,” “but now,” “in Christ,” Spirit, flesh, law, faith and righteousness without assuming each term has one meaning everywhere. Map Abraham, Adam, Israel and olive-tree images. Read chapters 14–15 as a concrete communal application rather than an appendix. Note the many named coworkers in chapter 16 when assessing Paul’s mission network.

Context and interpretation note

Romans has shaped major disputes about justification, law, election, Israel, sexuality, government and women’s ministry. Traditions define key Greek terms and Paul’s opponents differently. Romans 13 must not be used to demand obedience to abuse or erase prophetic critique, and chapters 9–11 must not support antisemitism. The quiz tests explicit claims and argumentative sequence while avoiding one denominational system as the only factual reading.
