What is Philemon about?

Philemon is a brief personal appeal involving Paul, Philemon and Onesimus within Roman slavery. Paul thanks Philemon, calls the enslaved Onesimus his child and brother, asks that he be received as Paul himself, offers to cover debt and seeks a response grounded in love rather than compulsion.

This guide treats Philemon as a situated argument rather than a collection of slogans. The six questions sample distinct units, while the map locates every answer. Watch repetitions, contrasts, connective words and shifts of audience. Verify an answer across more than one verse whenever its wording depends on the reasoning before or after it.

A four-part reading map

  1. 1–7 — greeting and thanksgiving: house church, love, faith, shared life and refreshed hearts
  2. 8–12 — appeal rather than command: aged prisoner, Onesimus as child and transformed usefulness
  3. 13–17 — voluntary goodness and new kinship: desired service, consent, separation and brotherhood
  4. 18–25 — debt, partnership and confidence: Paul’s guarantee, refreshed heart, lodging and greetings

Sample questions and answers

Use each reference as an entry point and read the whole unit before selecting an answer.

In whose house did a church meet?
Answer: Philemon’s household
Reference: Philemon 1–3

Apphia and Archippus are also addressed in the greeting.

For what did Paul thank God?
Answer: Philemon’s love and faith
Reference: Philemon 4–7

Reports of refreshed believers prepare the later request.

Whom did Paul call his child?
Answer: Onesimus
Reference: Philemon 8–12

The relationship was formed during Paul’s imprisonment.

How did Paul want Philemon’s goodness to operate?
Answer: Voluntarily, not by compulsion
Reference: Philemon 13–14

Paul sends Onesimus back rather than acting without consent.

How should Onesimus be received?
Answer: No longer merely as a slave, but as a beloved brother
Reference: Philemon 15–17

Paul also asks that he be welcomed as Paul himself.

Who offered to repay any debt?
Answer: Paul
Reference: Philemon 18–19

The handwritten guarantee strengthens the appeal.

How to study Philemon

Read Philemon as a complete letter before isolating memorable lines. Summarise each of the four mapped sections and note who speaks, which problem is answered and what practical consequence follows. Trace connections among greeting and thanksgiving, appeal rather than command, voluntary goodness and new kinship, debt, partnership and confidence. For every question, verify the full paragraph, compare transitions and distinguish assertion, quotation, metaphor and exhortation. Record repeated terms and named coworkers, then explain how the closing material returns to concerns introduced near the opening. This method keeps recall, argument, social setting and application in conversation.

Context and interpretation note

The letter does not explicitly record Onesimus’s legal outcome, and interpreters debate whether Paul seeks manumission. Roman slavery was coercive and should not be softened into ordinary employment. Responsible study names the power imbalance, hears the appeal toward brotherhood and refuses to use the letter to defend slavery or forced reconciliation.
