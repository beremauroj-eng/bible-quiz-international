What is Judges about?

Judges portrays a fragmented period between Joshua and Israel’s monarchy. Repeated cycles of unfaithfulness, oppression, cries for help and deliverance frame stories of local leaders, while the final chapters show growing social and moral disorder.

A three-part reading map

  1. Judges 1–3:6 — setting the pattern: incomplete occupation, the next generation and the recurring cycle explained.
  2. Judges 3:7–16 — major deliverers: Othniel, Ehud, Deborah and Barak, Gideon, Jephthah and Samson.
  3. Judges 17–21 — breakdown: Micah’s shrine, the migration of Dan and the violence involving Gibeah and Benjamin.

Sample questions and answers

Notice where each answer sits in the downward movement of the book rather than treating every judge as an interchangeable hero.

Which left-handed judge delivered Israel from Eglon king of Moab?
Answer: Ehud
Reference: Judges 3:12–30

Ehud concealed a sword and delivered a message to Eglon before Israel defeated Moab.

Which prophetess judged Israel and summoned Barak?
Answer: Deborah
Reference: Judges 4:4–10

Deborah instructed Barak concerning Sisera and accompanied him when he insisted she go.

What sign involving wool did Gideon request?
Answer: A fleece wet with dew while the ground was dry, then the reverse
Reference: Judges 6:36–40

Gideon requested the paired signs before the campaign against Midian.

How many men remained in Gideon’s force for the attack on Midian?
Answer: Three hundred
Reference: Judges 7:1–8

The force was reduced in stages before the night attack.

To whom did Samson reveal the explanation of his strength?
Answer: Delilah
Reference: Judges 16:15–20

Samson told her that his hair had never been cut, after which she arranged for it to be shaved.

What repeated statement closes the book?
Answer: There was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in their own eyes
Reference: Judges 21:25

The closing line repeats a refrain used to interpret the disorder of the final chapters.

How to study Judges

For each deliverer, record the oppressor, the cry or commission, the method of deliverance and the aftermath. Then compare the opening explanation in Judges 2 with the final refrain. Pay attention to how the narratives become more complex and disturbing; the book often reports actions without presenting every action as a model to imitate.

Translation and interpretation note

The Hebrew title refers to leaders who exercised judgment or governance, not only courtroom officials. Character descriptions can be translated differently, and interpreters disagree over how the narrator evaluates some vows, violence and signs. Questions distinguish what the story explicitly says from conclusions readers may draw about a character’s motives or moral standing.
