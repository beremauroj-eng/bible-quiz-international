What is 2 Kings about?

2 Kings continues the parallel histories of Israel and Judah. Elisha succeeds Elijah, rulers rise and fall under prophetic evaluation, Samaria falls to Assyria, reforms occur in Judah, and Jerusalem is finally captured by Babylon.

A four-part reading map

  1. 2 Kings 1–8 — Elijah and Elisha: succession, prophetic signs, the Shunammite household, Naaman and conflicts with Aram.
  2. 2 Kings 9–17 — Jehu to Samaria’s fall: overthrow of Ahab’s house, dynastic changes and Assyrian conquest of the northern kingdom.
  3. 2 Kings 18–20 — Hezekiah: reform, the Assyrian threat, Jerusalem’s deliverance and the king’s illness.
  4. 2 Kings 21–25 — Judah’s final decades: Manasseh, Josiah’s reform, Babylonian domination, Jerusalem’s fall and exile.

Sample questions and answers

Use the questions to connect prophetic episodes with the political sequence of the two kingdoms.

Who succeeded Elijah as the leading prophet in the opening chapters?
Answer: Elisha
Reference: 2 Kings 2:1–15

Elisha witnessed Elijah’s departure and took up Elijah’s fallen mantle.

How many times was Naaman instructed to wash in the Jordan?
Answer: Seven times
Reference: 2 Kings 5:1–14

Naaman initially objected but followed Elisha’s instruction and was cleansed.

Which empire captured Samaria and exiled the northern kingdom?
Answer: Assyria
Reference: 2 Kings 17:1–6

Samaria fell after a siege during the reign of Hoshea.

Which king of Judah faced the Assyrian invasion led by Sennacherib?
Answer: Hezekiah
Reference: 2 Kings 18:13–19:37

Hezekiah received threats, sought Isaiah and prayed in the temple.

During whose reign was a scroll of the law found in the temple?
Answer: Josiah’s reign
Reference: 2 Kings 22:1–13

Hilkiah found the scroll during temple repairs, and Shaphan read it to the king.

Which empire destroyed Jerusalem and carried many people into exile?
Answer: Babylon
Reference: 2 Kings 24:18–25:21

Nebuchadnezzar’s forces besieged the city, destroyed major buildings and deported inhabitants.

How to study 2 Kings

Keep a ruler chart with separate columns for Israel and Judah until the fall of Samaria, then continue Judah alone. Add prophets and foreign empires beside the relevant reigns. Compare reform accounts with what follows; the book’s final outcome is explained through a long cumulative history, not a single isolated event.

Translation and chronology note

Some kings have alternate names, and regnal synchronisms are calculated differently by modern chronologies. Assyrian and Babylonian records can illuminate the period but do not replace close reading of the biblical narrative. Questions cite the book’s own sequence and avoid overstating uncertain date conversions or disputed historical identifications.
