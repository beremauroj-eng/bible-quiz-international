What is Ruth about?

Ruth is a compact family narrative set during the days of the judges. Famine, bereavement and migration give way to gleaning, loyal action, legal negotiation and restoration. The closing genealogy connects Ruth and Boaz’s family with David.

A chapter-by-chapter reading map

  1. Ruth 1 — loss and return: Naomi’s family in Moab, three deaths, Ruth’s commitment and arrival in Bethlehem.
  2. Ruth 2 — provision in the field: gleaning, Boaz’s protection and Naomi’s recognition of a family redeemer.
  3. Ruth 3 — appeal at the threshing floor: Naomi’s plan, Ruth’s request and Boaz’s promise to act lawfully.
  4. Ruth 4 — redemption and restoration: negotiation at the gate, marriage, birth of Obed and genealogy.

Sample questions and answers

These questions follow the narrative order so names, places and legal actions remain connected.

From which land did Naomi return to Bethlehem?
Answer: Moab
Reference: Ruth 1:1–6, 22

Naomi had gone to Moab with Elimelech and their sons during a famine in Judah.

What commitment did Ruth make to Naomi?
Answer: To go where Naomi went and make Naomi’s people and God her own
Reference: Ruth 1:16–17

The speech explains why Ruth continues to Bethlehem rather than returning to Moab.

In whose field did Ruth happen to glean?
Answer: Boaz’s field
Reference: Ruth 2:1–3

Boaz is introduced as a relative of Elimelech before Ruth arrives in his field.

What family role did Naomi say Boaz could fulfil?
Answer: A redeemer, or kinsman-redeemer
Reference: Ruth 2:20

The statement connects Boaz’s kindness with a recognised family responsibility.

Why did Boaz first approach another man at the town gate?
Answer: That man was a nearer redeemer
Reference: Ruth 3:12–13; 4:1–6

Boaz followed the order of family responsibility before taking the role himself.

Who was the son of Ruth and Boaz, later named as David’s grandfather?
Answer: Obed
Reference: Ruth 4:13–17

The women identify Obed in relation to Naomi, and the genealogy links him to Jesse and David.

How to study Ruth

Track every movement between Moab, Bethlehem, field, threshing floor and town gate. Note who initiates each act of care and how earlier losses are answered by later scenes. Read the genealogy as part of the narrative conclusion, not as an unrelated appendix.

Translation and cultural note

The Hebrew terms often rendered “steadfast kindness” and “redeemer” carry relational and legal associations that no single English word captures fully. Customs involving gleaning, land and family obligation belong to the story’s ancient setting. Questions identify actions stated in Ruth and avoid pretending that every detail of the legal background is explained within the four chapters.
