What is Amos about?

Amos delivers prophetic messages to the prosperous northern kingdom of Israel while Jeroboam II reigns. Beginning with judgments on surrounding nations, the book turns the same moral scrutiny on Israel. Exploitation of the poor, corrupt courts, luxurious indifference and worship disconnected from justice make military and religious confidence dangerously false.

A four-part reading map

  1. Amos 1–2 — the nations and Israel: repeated “for three transgressions, and for four” oracles circle toward Judah and then an extended charge against Israel.
  2. Amos 3–4 — summoned to account: prophetic necessity, wealthy houses, oppressed people, failed warnings and preparation to meet God.
  3. Amos 5–6 — seek, lament and beware: calls to seek good, rejection of unjust worship, redefined day of the Lord and woe to complacent elites.
  4. Amos 7–9 — visions and conclusion: locusts, fire, plumb line, conflict with Amaziah, summer fruit, altar judgment and a final restoration promise.

Sample questions and answers

Connect Amos’s famous worship statements to the economic and judicial practices named around them; justice is not an abstract slogan in this book.

What occupations did Amos identify as his work?
Answer: Herdsman or shepherd and dresser of sycamore-fig trees
Reference: Amos 1:1; 7:14–15

Amos says the Lord took him from tending the flock and sent him to prophesy to Israel.

What does Amos call to roll down like waters?
Answer: Justice, with righteousness like an ever-flowing stream
Reference: Amos 5:21–24

The call follows rejection of festivals, assemblies, offerings and songs without right conduct.

How does Amos challenge those who long for the day of the Lord?
Answer: He says it will be darkness rather than light for them
Reference: Amos 5:18–20

The passage uses images of escaping one danger only to meet another.

What measuring tool appeared in Amos’s third vision?
Answer: A plumb line
Reference: Amos 7:7–9

The vision announces that Israel will be measured and the sanctuaries and dynasty judged.

Which priest told Amos to leave Bethel and return to Judah?
Answer: Amaziah
Reference: Amos 7:10–17

Amaziah reported Amos to the king and called Bethel the king’s sanctuary.

What did the basket of summer fruit signify?
Answer: That the end had come upon Israel
Reference: Amos 8:1–3

A Hebrew wordplay connects “summer fruit” with “end,” followed by a scene of mourning.

How to study Amos

Map the opening oracles in geographic order and observe how listeners are drawn into agreement before Israel is addressed. Build a list of specific injustices: debt slavery, dishonest trade, bribery, court failure and luxury without concern. Mark every “hear,” “seek,” “woe” and “therefore.” In the vision sequence, note when Amos intercedes successfully, when judgment becomes fixed and how the conflict at Bethel interrupts the visions.

Context and interpretation note

Amos speaks into eighth-century BCE economic, legal and cultic systems; modern application requires understanding those structures rather than using isolated lines as partisan slogans. The book’s final restoration passage, relationship to earlier judgment and references to David’s booth are interpreted differently. Hebrew wordplay sometimes disappears in translation. The quiz tests named practices, visions and explicit explanations while avoiding claims that wealth alone proves guilt or hardship proves virtue.
