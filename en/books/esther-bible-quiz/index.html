What is Esther about?

Esther is a court narrative set in the Persian Empire. A royal banquet and the removal of Queen Vashti lead to Esther becoming queen, while Mordecai’s refusal to honour Haman develops into a decree threatening Jewish communities. Risky intervention, competing edicts and carefully staged reversals lead to deliverance and the establishment of Purim.

A four-part reading map

  1. Esther 1–2 — palace and selection: Ahasuerus’s banquets, Vashti’s removal, Esther’s selection and Mordecai’s discovery of a plot.
  2. Esther 3–4 — decree and decision: Haman’s anger, casting of the pur, the extermination decree, mourning and Esther’s commitment to approach the king.
  3. Esther 5–7 — banquets and reversal: Esther’s invitations, Haman’s gallows, the king’s sleepless night, Mordecai’s honour and Haman’s exposure.
  4. Esther 8–10 — defence and remembrance: a new royal edict, Jewish self-defence, celebration, Purim and Mordecai’s prominence.

Sample questions and answers

Track who knows Esther’s identity at each stage and when apparently minor earlier events return to change the direction of the story.

Who raised Esther after the death of her parents?
Answer: Mordecai, her older cousin or relative
Reference: Esther 2:5–7

Mordecai took Hadassah, also called Esther, as his own daughter.

What did Esther initially keep secret at Mordecai’s instruction?
Answer: Her people and family background
Reference: Esther 2:10, 20

Her Jewish identity was not disclosed during her selection and early time in the palace.

Why did Haman become furious with Mordecai?
Answer: Mordecai would not bow down or pay him honour
Reference: Esther 3:1–6

Haman then sought a decree against all Mordecai’s people throughout the empire.

What did Esther ask the Jewish community in Susa to do before she approached the king?
Answer: Fast for her for three days and nights
Reference: Esther 4:15–17

Esther and her attendants would also fast before she entered the king’s presence uninvited.

What record was read to the king during his sleepless night?
Answer: The record of Mordecai exposing the assassination plot
Reference: Esther 6:1–3

The discovery that Mordecai had not been honoured sets up the public reversal involving Haman.

What festival commemorated the community’s reversal from threatened destruction to deliverance?
Answer: Purim
Reference: Esther 9:20–28

The name recalls the pur, or lot, that Haman had cast when choosing the date for his plan.

How to study Esther

Make a scene chart for banquets, decrees, messengers and changes of clothing or status. Note repetitions such as “the king and Haman sat down to drink” against the city’s confusion, then watch how later scenes reverse earlier positions. Record what each character knows, because suspense often depends on delayed disclosure. Read chapters 8–9 carefully rather than ending the story with Haman’s death; the first decree still requires a political response.

Context and interpretation note

The book uses Persian court titles, names and administrative geography that translations spell differently. It also portrays imperial power, threatened genocide and violent self-defence, subjects requiring historical and ethical care. God is not explicitly named in the Hebrew text, although readers draw different conclusions about providence and hidden action. Quiz questions test the narrative’s stated events and literary reversals without presenting one theological inference or modern political analogy as an uncontested fact.
