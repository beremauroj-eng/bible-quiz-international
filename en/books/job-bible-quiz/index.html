What is Job about?

Job explores undeserved suffering through a prose framework and extended poetic debate. After sudden losses, Job disputes his friends’ explanations and repeatedly seeks an answer from God. Elihu adds another series of speeches before God addresses Job from the whirlwind, shifting attention from a simple theory of retribution to the scale and complexity of creation.

A four-part reading map

  1. Job 1–2 — testing and loss: Job’s character, scenes in the heavenly council, loss of possessions and children, bodily suffering and the arrival of three friends.
  2. Job 3–31 — lament and debate: Job curses the day of his birth, Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar respond in cycles, and Job maintains his complaint and integrity.
  3. Job 32–37 — Elihu’s speeches: a younger observer challenges both Job and the three friends while emphasising divine instruction and greatness.
  4. Job 38–42 — whirlwind and outcome: God questions Job about creation, Job responds, the friends are corrected and Job’s fortunes are restored.

Sample questions and answers

Identify who is speaking before treating any statement as the book’s conclusion; much of Job records arguments that the narrative later evaluates.

How is Job described at the beginning of the book?
Answer: Blameless and upright, fearing God and turning away from evil
Reference: Job 1:1

The description establishes the tension between Job’s character and the suffering that follows.

What motive did the accuser question when discussing Job’s faithfulness?
Answer: Whether Job feared God only because he was protected and prospered
Reference: Job 1:9–11

The challenge claims that removing Job’s blessings would reveal self-interested loyalty.

Which three friends came to mourn with Job after hearing of his troubles?
Answer: Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar
Reference: Job 2:11–13

They first sat silently with Job for seven days before the poetic dialogue began.

Who was the younger speaker who waited until Job and the three friends had finished?
Answer: Elihu son of Barakel
Reference: Job 32:1–6

Elihu explains that he delayed speaking because the others were older.

From what did the Lord answer Job?
Answer: From the whirlwind or storm
Reference: Job 38:1–3

The response begins with questions about knowledge and the foundations of the earth.

What was Job instructed to do for the friends whom God corrected?
Answer: Pray for them as they offered sacrifices
Reference: Job 42:7–10

God accepted Job’s prayer, and the narrative then describes the restoration of his fortunes.

How to study Job

Colour-code the speakers and mark each transition, because a memorable line from a friend is not automatically the narrator’s verdict. Track repeated themes—innocence, justice, wisdom, creation, knowledge and the desire for an advocate—across the debate. Compare the prose opening and ending with the poetic centre. When God speaks, list the creatures and natural processes mentioned and ask how the questions widen the discussion beyond the friends’ narrow cause-and-effect explanations.

Context and interpretation note

Job contains difficult Hebrew poetry, rare words and images whose translation varies. The identity and role of “the accuser,” the structure of the debate, Elihu’s contribution and the meaning of Job’s final response are interpreted differently. The speeches also include claims that God later rejects as inadequate. This quiz identifies speakers, sequence and explicit narrative outcomes; it does not present every quoted claim as divine teaching or reduce real suffering to a formula.
